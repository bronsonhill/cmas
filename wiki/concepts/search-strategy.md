# Search Strategy

A search strategy is the rule an agent follows when looking for something whose location it
does not know — how it moves through space and how it responds to what it finds.

## How it works

The strategies raised in the Week 1 foraging exercise, and the conditions each requires:

- **Random walk.** Move in random directions. With no prior information this is the
  sensible default; a large step size helps cover ground. This is the zero-information
  baseline.
- **Greedy / cluster-based.** On finding a target, search intensively nearby. Depends
  entirely on targets being **clustered** — it exploits spatial correlation. Excellent for
  mushrooms that fruit in groups, useless for truffles, which do not cluster.
- **Spiral.** Systematic outward coverage from a starting point. Guarantees coverage but
  must start somewhere specific, and the spiral's tightness must be matched to the sensing
  radius (see [[agent-sensing]]).
- **Zigzag / transect.** Systematic sweep across an area; the practical alternative to a
  spiral when there is no natural centre.
- **Cue-directed.** Search for a correlated feature rather than the target — look for host
  trees, fallen logs, or leaf litter, then search locally. Effectively a learned prior about
  where targets occur.
- **Multi-agent search.** Several searchers in parallel: covers more ground in less time,
  at the cost of more resources — and, as raised in class, the coordination problem of
  whether searchers share what they find.

The central lesson is that **no strategy is best in the abstract**. Which one wins depends
on the target's spatial distribution, the sensing mechanism, and the available prior
information — all of which are fixed by the [[research-question]]. This is why narrowing
"mushrooms" to "truffles" mattered so much: it changed the answer.

## Why it matters

Search is a recurring agent behaviour across ABM application domains — foraging animals,
consumers searching a market, robots exploring terrain. It is also a clean illustration of
how agent-level rules must be justified by system properties rather than chosen for
elegance, and it connects directly to [[optimisation]] versus [[satisficing]]: a
"good enough" search that terminates early may beat an exhaustive one.
