# Self-Organisation

Self-organisation is the process by which a system spontaneously develops nontrivial
macroscopic structure or behaviour over time, without an external controller imposing that
structure.

## How it works

Where [[emergence]] is about *scale* — a macroscopic property not simply reducible to
microscopic rules — self-organisation adds *time*: it names the dynamical process by which
an emergent pattern actually comes to exist as the system evolves. A flock coalescing out
of scattered individuals, a market price settling out of uncoordinated buying and selling,
a termite mound rising out of individually blind insects — all are self-organising in this
sense, and each is also an instance of [[emergence]], but self-organisation is the
*process*, emergence is the *relationship between scales* that process produces.

Self-organisation can look like it violates the second law of thermodynamics, since
entropy of a closed system should only increase, yet self-organising systems appear to
spontaneously generate order. The resolution is that these systems are open: they exchange
energy or information with their environment, and the local increase in order is paid for
by entropy production elsewhere. Self-organisation therefore gives a dynamical account of
where emergent properties come from, rather than treating them as static labels.

## Why it matters

Self-organisation is one of the two concepts (with [[emergence]]) that Sayama identifies as
running through essentially every subarea of complex systems science, and it is the reason
[[decentralisation|decentralised]] systems with only local rules — [[flocking]],
[[cellular-automaton|cellular automata]], market dynamics — can still produce coherent,
reproducible global structure without a designer or controller.
