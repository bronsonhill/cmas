# Epstein (2008) — Why Model?

## Overview

A four-page essay by [[joshua-epstein]] in the *Journal of Artificial Societies and Social
Simulation* (vol. 11, no. 4, article 12), adapted from his 2008 Bastille Day keynote to the
Second World Congress on Social Simulation. Open access. It is an argument, not a method
paper, and it is aimed at two audiences at once: modellers who are asked to justify what
they do, and critics who assume prediction is the only defensible goal.

The opening move is that the choice is never whether to model. Anyone who ventures a
projection about how an epidemic, war or migration will unfold is running a model already
— an implicit one, whose assumptions are hidden, whose internal consistency is untested,
and whose logical consequences are unknown. The real choice is between implicit and
explicit models. Epstein's retort to "can you validate your model?" is "can you validate
yours?"; at least an explicit model can in principle be calibrated against data, replicated
by others, and swept across its parameter space to find where the results are sensitive
([[sensitivity-analysis]]).

The centrepiece is a list of sixteen reasons to build a model other than prediction, and
the accompanying insistence that explanation does not imply prediction. Plate tectonics
explains earthquakes without predicting when the next one hits; electrostatics explains
lightning without saying where the next bolt lands. Epstein's own contribution to that
gap is [[generative-explanation]]: showing that a macroscopic regularity emerges from
heterogeneous agents following plausible local rules ([[emergence]],
[[agent-based-model]]).

He also attacks the naïve inductivism that says models come after data. Theory routinely
precedes data collection and tells you what data to collect — Maxwell's equations implied
radio waves before anyone looked for them. And the essay closes somewhere unexpected for a
methods paper: modelling enforces a habit of mind Epstein calls *militant ignorance*, an
iron commitment to "I don't know", where beliefs rest on evidence rather than authority.

## Notable claims / results

- The sixteen reasons: explain; guide data collection; illuminate core dynamics; suggest
  dynamical analogies; discover new questions; promote a scientific habit of mind; bound
  outcomes to plausible ranges; illuminate core uncertainties; offer crisis options in
  near-real time; demonstrate tradeoffs and suggest efficiencies; challenge the robustness
  of prevailing theory through perturbations; expose prevailing wisdom as incompatible with
  available data; train practitioners; discipline the policy dialogue; educate the public;
  reveal the apparently simple to be complex and vice versa.
- Models do not remove the need for judgment in the policy sphere. By exposing tradeoffs,
  uncertainties and sensitivities they make unavoidable judgments more considered.
- Lotka-Volterra, Hooke's Law and the Kermack-McKendrick epidemic equations are all wrong in
  an engineering sense and all still taught, because they capture qualitative behaviours of
  overarching interest — predator-prey cycles, the nonlinear threshold nature of epidemics,
  herd immunity ([[herd-immunity-threshold]], [[sir-model]], [[tipping-point]]).
- Analogies between formally identical models are "headlights in dark unexplored territory":
  if revolutions or the adoption of innovations unfold like epidemics, existing epidemic
  theory transfers to the new field.
