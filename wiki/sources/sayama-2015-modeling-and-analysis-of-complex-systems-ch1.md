# Sayama (2015), Introduction to the Modeling and Analysis of Complex Systems — Chapter 1

## Overview

Chapter 1 of Hiroki Sayama's open-access textbook (Open SUNY Textbooks, 2015,
CC BY-NC-SA) is the source of the working definition of [[complex-system]] used directly
in [[w04a-complex-systems-and-emergence]]. Sayama opens by defining the term outright
rather than building up to it: complex systems are networks of interacting, typically
nonlinearly-coupled components that may arise through [[self-organisation]], sit between
fully regular and fully random, and produce [[emergence|emergent]] behaviour at
macroscopic scales.

The chapter's organising move is to define complex systems by what they are *not*.
Weaver's 1948 distinction between "problems of simplicity" (few tightly-coupled
components, small number of degrees of freedom — e.g. rigid-body mechanics) and "problems
of disorganized complexity" (many fully independent components — e.g. an ideal gas, coin
tosses, both well handled by classical statistics) leaves a large middle ground Weaver
called "problems of organized complexity". Complex systems science is Sayama's name for
the field that fills that gap: systems with many *interdependent* components, where
neither treating the system as one tightly-coupled unit nor treating components as
statistically independent is adequate.

Sayama then separates two ideas that are often conflated. **Emergence** concerns *scale*:
following Yaneer Bar-Yam, it is a nontrivial relationship between a system's properties at
microscopic and macroscopic scales — a macroscopic property (a dog being "alive", a person
being "conscious") that resists simple reduction to the microscopic rules producing it.
**Self-organisation** concerns *time* (in addition to scale): a system is self-organising
when it spontaneously develops nontrivial macroscopic structure or behaviour as time
progresses, in apparent tension with the second law of thermodynamics — a tension resolved
by noting self-organising systems are open, exchanging energy with their environment
rather than being closed. Self-organisation is offered as a *dynamical* explanation for
emergent properties: it describes the process by which the macroscopic pattern that
emergence names actually comes to exist.

The chapter closes with a map of the field into seven topical clusters, grouped as three
historical roots and four more recent research fronts. The historical roots are
**nonlinear dynamics** (from Newton and Leibniz's calculus through 20th-century recognition
that nonlinear systems can be chaotic, and hence analytically unsolvable — one of several
origins of the idea of complexity), **systems theory** (WWII-era engineering-driven work:
Turing's computability, Wiener's cybernetics, Shannon's information theory), and **game
theory** (von Neumann and Morgenstern's Cold War-era formalisation of strategic decision-
making, later applied across economics, political science, biology). The four more recent
clusters are **pattern formation** (spatial self-organisation, exemplified by cellular
automata — von Neumann and Ulam, 1940s — and by partial differential equation models),
**evolution and adaptation** (biological evolution plus its computational analogues:
evolutionary computation, artificial neural networks, machine learning), **networks**
(graph theory since Euler, reinvigorated by large-scale relational data), and **collective
behaviour** (social dynamics and swarm-style coordination, similarly reinvigorated by data
availability).

## Topics covered (revision checklist)

- The chapter's opening definition of "complex systems" (components, nonlinear
  interaction, possible self-organisation, position between regular and random, emergent
  macroscopic behaviour)
- Weaver's three-way distinction: problems of simplicity, problems of disorganized
  complexity, problems of organized complexity
- Why an ideal gas and fixed coin tosses are not complex systems (statistical independence)
- Why rigid-body mechanics is not a complex system (near-total coupling, few degrees of
  freedom)
- Definition of emergence (Bar-Yam): nontrivial relationship between micro- and macro-
  scale properties
- Definition of self-organisation: spontaneous development of macroscopic structure/
  behaviour over time
- Why self-organisation does not violate the second law of thermodynamics (open vs closed
  systems)
- The distinction between emergence (about scale) and self-organisation (about scale
  *and* time)
- The seven topical clusters of complex systems science and their historical grouping:
  nonlinear dynamics, systems theory, game theory (roots); pattern formation, evolution
  and adaptation, networks, collective behaviour (recent fronts)
- Cellular automata's origin (von Neumann and Ulam, Los Alamos, 1940s) as the chapter's
  own example of a pattern-formation model

## Notable claims / results

- Sayama's working definition: "Complex systems are networks made of a number of
  components that interact with each other, typically in a nonlinear fashion. Complex
  systems may arise and evolve through self-organization, such that they are neither
  completely regular nor completely random, permitting the development of emergent
  behavior at macroscopic scales."
- Emergence and self-organisation are frequently conflated in the literature but are
  conceptually distinct: emergence is about scale, self-organisation is about scale and
  time together.
- Nonlinearity is a necessary condition for chaotic behaviour; chaotic systems generally
  have no available analytical solution, which the chapter names as one of the origins of
  the idea of complexity.
